# Reviewing 8nOut's German Privacy Policy / Terms of Service translation

This page is about one specific thing: checking the German translation of
two legal documents — 8nOut's Privacy Policy and Terms of Service. It is
**not** the general translation-correction lane described in
[CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md), and finishing a review here doesn't
work the same way that one does. Please read this whole page before you
start, even if you've already read CONTRIBUTING.md — the two are separate,
on purpose.

## What this covers

Only two files, only German, only for now:

- `privacy-policy.de.md`
- `terms-of-service.de.md`

If you speak German and want to help with everyday app text instead —
buttons, Help Centre answers, onboarding copy — that's the general lane in
[CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md), and being a native speaker is genuinely
enough there. This page is for something narrower and heavier: the two
documents that describe, in a way a member can actually rely on, what
8nOut does with their data and what they've agreed to.

**A correction accepted here does not mean the document is cleared for
publication.** Even after a reviewer signs off, this German text stays
unpublished and internal until 8nOut's own legal review closes it out.
Nothing you review here goes live just because your review was accepted.

## What you'd actually be reading

The German text posted for review is Claude's AI-drafted best-effort
translation of the English original. Nobody has checked it yet — not a
native speaker, not a translator, not a lawyer. It is **not** 8nOut's
actual German Privacy Policy or Terms of Service, in any sense you or
anyone else can rely on. It isn't in effect anywhere. Treat it exactly
like a first draft that happens to already be typed out, because that's
what it is.

## Who this is for

Being a native German speaker is a great start, but on its own it isn't
enough for this lane — that's the whole difference between this page and
the general one. Legal documents use words that carry specific legal
meaning, and a translation that reads perfectly naturally can still get
one of those words wrong in a way a general fluency check won't catch.

Before you start, we'll ask you to tell us — privately, see below — why
you're positioned to catch that kind of thing. Any of the following
counts:

- Professional translation experience, especially with legal or official
  documents
- A law degree, or legal-practice background, in Germany or elsewhere
- Sworn or certified-translator status — Germany's own version of this is
  a *vereidigter* or *öffentlich bestellter Übersetzer* (a court-sworn or
  publicly-appointed translator), which is one clean example of what we
  mean, not the only one that counts
- Something equivalent we haven't listed — tell us what it is and let us
  judge

If none of that describes you but you'd still like to flag something you
noticed, you're welcome to open an issue and say so plainly. We'll read
it, but it won't count as the sign-off this document needs.

## What signing off here actually means

This isn't a quick "that sentence sounds a bit off" fix. Reviewing a legal
document means reading it the way you'd read a contract — checking that a
specific legal concept is rendered with the specific word German law uses
for it, not just a word that sounds close. Saying yes to this is a bigger
commitment of time and judgment than the general translation lane asks
for, and we'd rather you go in knowing that than find out halfway through.

## One thing we already know to check

There's a specific clause we know is easy to get wrong, and we'd like you
to check it specifically rather than assume it's fine: the section
describing how a member grants access to their **precise home-table
address** (in the Privacy Policy). That access is granted on a contract
basis, not a consent basis — so the German text should **not** use
*Einwilligung* (or another consent-specific term) to describe it. If it
does, that's exactly the kind of error we're asking you to catch: it would
tell a German reader that withdrawing consent stops the process, which
isn't how this actually works. Please confirm, one way or the other,
whether this clause is correct — even if everything else looks fine.

## How to submit what you find

Open a **[Legal document translation review issue](../../issues/new?template=legal-document-translation-review.yml)**.
It asks which document you reviewed, what you found, and specifically asks
about the clause above.

Then — separately, and privately — send us your name and the competence
basis from the list above. See the next section for why that's split into
two places.

## Why your name doesn't go in the public issue

Two different things happen when you help with this:

1. **Your public GitHub activity** — the issue you open, and an optional
   credit line in `CONTRIBUTORS.md` if you want one — stays exactly as
   pseudonymous as you'd like, the same as the general translation lane.
   Nobody needs your real name to read or comment on a GitHub issue.
2. **The actual compliance record** — that a named, competent person
   checked this document, on a specific date — has to resolve to a real
   person if it's going to mean anything later. That part is not public.

So: send your real name and your competence basis (from the list above)
privately, to `translations@8nout.com` — not as a comment on the issue.
We'll keep it on file as the record that a specific, named, qualified
person reviewed this document, and nothing more. It's never posted,
quoted, or attached to your GitHub handle anywhere.

## What happens after you submit

Someone at 8nOut checks that your stated competence basis is a reasonable
fit, takes a second look at anything you flagged (a second pair of eyes is
part of how a document this important gets signed off, same as it would
be with any professional translator), and records the review internally.
If everything holds up, the German draft moves one step closer to being
an actual, publishable translation — but it still isn't published the
moment your review is accepted. That happens later, through 8nOut's own
legal-review process, separately.
